## Thesis and title

**Thesis:** a small INT8 accelerator on the KV260 whose performance is exactly predictable.
Its analytical cycle model, its RTL and the real board agree to the cycle on every layer, and
its outputs are bit-exact against the reference.

We do not compete on raw throughput. Our strengths are exactness, determinism, honest
measurement and reproducibility. Every section should support that.

**Comparison position (final):** against the AMD DPU we are 1.5× faster on LeNet-5 at half
its energy per image, and 1.6× slower on CIFAR-10 at equal energy, while the loaded design
adds about 0.23 W at idle against the DPU's 1.15 W. Against the best CPU (ONNX Runtime
INT8, 4 threads) we are 2.1–3.1× faster and use 5.5–7× less energy. Never claim to beat the
DPU overall; lead with exactness, zero-spread compute latency and energy, and state the
DPU's CIFAR-10 latency win plainly. End-to-end spread comes from the Python host, so
the determinism claim applies to accelerator compute only.

## Section-by-section content

Each section lists what it must say. "Now" means writable today from laptop results;
"Board" means it waits for measurements.

### I. Introduction (Now)

1. Problem: FPGA CNN accelerator papers often report modeled or simulated
   performance; the gap to real hardware is rarely quantified.
2. Why it matters: predictable latency matters for embedded and real-time use; a model
   you can trust enables design-space exploration without building every design.
3. Our approach: build the model first, fix its constants before simulation, then hold RTL
   and board to it cycle for cycle.
4. Contributions list (from the Thesis section).
5. One sentence on scope: small networks by design; the claim is exactness, not peak
   throughput.

### II. Related work (Now, needs literature search)

1. FPGA CNN accelerators (output-stationary and systolic designs, small-network edge
   accelerators).
2. Analytical performance models for accelerators and how closely they have matched
   hardware.
3. Vendor IP: the AMD DPU on Kria.
4. Verification and reproducibility practice in FPGA research.

End with one sentence on the gap we fill: few works show model, RTL and board agreeing
exactly. Do not cite anything we haven't read.

### III. Architecture (Now)

1. Numeric contract: signed INT8, zero-point 0, per-tensor activation scales, per-output-
   channel weight scales, INT32 accumulation. Requantization = (acc + q_bias) × m,
   round-half-to-even shift by s, clip, ReLU. Final layer exports raw INT32 logits.
2. Datapath: 8×8 output-stationary outer-product array; rows = 8 consecutive output x
   positions, columns = 8 output channels; each PE is one DSP48E2. Double-buffered
   drain overlapped with the next tile. 8 requant lanes, fused 2×2 max-pool.
3. Memory: everything on-chip in BRAM, no DRAM traffic during inference. Ping-pong
   activation buffers banked by x mod 8 with a kx rotator for conflict-free reads; all
   weights resident.
4. Control: counter-only address generation (no multipliers), token pipeline, descriptor
   sequencer for up to 8 layers per start, pipelined config checker.
5. Interface: AXI-Lite CSR with cycle, MAC-active and stall counters, per-layer cycle
   registers, logits, BUILD_ID; loaded as a PYNQ overlay.
6. Supported shapes: VALID convolution, stride 1, kernel width ≤ 9 (host-enforced;
   verified at the boundary in RTL simulation).

### IV. Cycle model (Now)

1. Tiling order and tile count: T = ceil(OC/8) × OH × ceil(OW/8).
2. Per-layer cycles = T × K + C_PIPE (29), where K = IC × KH × KW; plus C_START (3) per
   job.
3. Constants were committed before the first RTL simulation.
4. Utilization analysis: 39.6% (LeNet) and 67.3% (CIFAR), lost mostly to partial 8-wide
   tiles; layers with a 1×1 output capped at 12.5%, 10-class final layers at 7.8%. This is the
   known output-stationary limit.
5. Ablation (model): a per-kernel-row schedule would take 1.83× (LeNet) and 2.16×
   (CIFAR) the cycles.

### V. Verification methodology (Now)

1. Requantization verified bit-exact on every non-saturating input (139.6M values
   exhaustive, 486.6M in total); 2 of 354 channels needed a 1-LSB adjustment of m
   (near-ties of the rounded multiplier).
2. Golden model matches the legacy reference on all 10k images of both networks.
3. Full-dataset RTL simulation: 20,000 images bit-exact and cycle-exact, Verilator cross-
   checked against xsim.
4. Random-shape validation: 300 seeded jobs in 13 categories; all 286 valid jobs bit-exact
   and cycle-exact; all 14 invalid jobs correctly refused.
5. Robustness: 100 back-to-back AXI jobs including a refused job and a mid-job soft reset;
   post-synthesis netlist simulation.
6. What verification found: random shapes revealed an unchecked kernel-width limit,
   now host-enforced. A follow-up audit boundary-tested the descriptor fields in 49 cases
   in two simulators and added host checks wherever the hardware checker has a gap.
   One or two sentences; it shows the method works.
7. Provenance: every number is script-generated from a clean commit with bitstream
   SHA256 and BUILD_ID; logs archived with hashes.

### VI. Experimental setup (Now)

1. Board: KV260 (K26 SOM), Kria Ubuntu 22.04, Kria-PYNQ. Clock: 250 MHz, set from the
   PS PLL and verified by read-back; all µs values use the read-back clock. The design also
   closes timing at 300 MHz, but the KV260's default PS PLL (999.99 MHz) cannot divide
   to it.
2. Implementation table (Table I).
3. Protocol: CPU governor fixed, cores pinned, warm-up discarded, ≥100 repeats, median
   with 95% CI, interleaved conditions.
4. Power: SOM-rail INA260 (VCC_SOM); idle, accelerator, control and CPU phases; energy
   = ΔP × time.
5. Baselines: Cortex-A53 (numpy INT8/FP32, ONNX Runtime FP32/INT8, 1 and 4 threads);
   AMD DPU with the prebuilt pynq_dpu 2.5 overlay.

### VII. Results (Board)

1. Three-way agreement: per-layer cycles model = RTL = board; spread across 10k images
   (Fig. 3).
2. Correctness: 0 logit mismatches on 10k images per net; accuracy equals golden.
3. Latency and throughput: compute-only and end-to-end p50/p95/p99; images/s.
4. Energy: µJ per image, GOPS/W, SOM-rail.
5. Comparison: CPU and DPU on the same board (Table II). Lead with determinism and
   latency, report throughput honestly.
6. Generality: random-shape predicted vs measured cycles (Fig. 4).
7. Clock sweep: cycles constant, latency ∝ 1/f, static vs dynamic power fit. One or two
   sentences.

### VIII. Conclusion and limitations (Mostly now)

1. Restate the claim with the headline numbers.
2. Limitations: small networks; VALID, stride 1 only; single 8×8 array; SOM-rail power
   includes the CPU driving the accelerator.
3. Future work: padding and stride, 16×16 array, larger networks.

## Writing rules

Reviewers reject papers for overclaiming more often than for modest results. These rules
keep every sentence defensible.

1. Label every number by origin: model, RTL simulation, post-implementation, or
   measured on the KV260.
2. Say "SOM-rail power (INA260)", never "accelerator power" or "FPGA power".
3. Say "projected" for anything model-only, such as the 16×16 array.
4. No number typed by hand: tables and figures come only from the pipeline.
5. No claim of "state of the art" or "fastest". Compare fairly and report where the DPU
   wins.
6. Every citation must be a paper someone on the team has actually read.
7. Report limitations in the paper itself; reviewers trust papers that state them.
8. Use LaTeX with IEEEtran (Overleaf works); keep the .tex in the repo under v2/paper/.
